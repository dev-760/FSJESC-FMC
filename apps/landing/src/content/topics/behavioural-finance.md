# Behavioural finance: the biases that cost you money

*For the members of the Financial Market Club, FSJES*

Traditional financial economics assumes that market participants are perfectly rational utility-maximizing agents: they update beliefs using Bayes' rule, evaluate risk objectively, and quickly arbitrage away any irrational price anomalies.

Behavioural finance, pioneered by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, demonstrates that human traders systematically violate these assumptions. Our cognitive biases do not produce random errors; they produce predictable, structured mistakes that cost investors significant capital.

## Core cognitive biases in trading

### 1. Loss Aversion (*Aversion a la perte*)
Under Kahneman and Tversky's Prospect Theory, humans feel the psychological pain of a monetary loss roughly twice as intensely as the pleasure derived from an equivalent financial gain.

Losing 10,000 MAD on a stock position generates roughly twice the emotional distress that making 10,000 MAD delivers in satisfaction. This causes traders to take irrational gambles to avoid locking in a loss, refusing to sell deteriorating assets in the desperate hope that they will return to even.

### 2. The Disposition Effect (*L'effet de disposition*)
The disposition effect is the single most robust empirical finding in investor behavior: **investors sell their winning investments too early and hold their losing investments far too long**.

On the Bourse de Casablanca, retail investors frequently exhibit this exact pattern:
- A stock climbs 8% above the purchase price: the investor rushes to sell to "lock in the profit" and feel the emotional satisfaction of being proven right.
- Another stock crashes 25% below purchase price: the investor refuses to sell, claiming "it is only a paper loss until I sell," retaining catastrophic risk in companies with deteriorating fundamentals.

The mathematical consequence for the portfolio is devastating: the investor systematically eliminates their strongest performers while concentrating their remaining capital in their worst-performing holdings.

### 3. Herding Behavior (*Comportement moutonnier*)
Humans are social animals wired to seek safety in consensus. In financial markets, herding manifests as buying whatever is making headlines or what peers are currently purchasing.

During major Moroccan Initial Public Offerings (IPOs) on the Bourse de Casablanca (such as the market debuts of Mutandis, Akdital, or Marsa Maroc), public excitement and media coverage frequently prompt retail participants to subscribe aggressively without analyzing valuation multiples or balance sheet risks. Conversely, during market downturns, collective panic induces retail dumping at the very bottom of the cycle.

### 4. Anchoring and Mental Accounting (*Ancrage et comptabilite mentale*)
- **Anchoring:** Fixating on an arbitrary historical price (typically the price you originally paid). If an investor bought a real estate stock at 80 MAD, they anchor on 80 MAD as the "fair value", even when severe corporate restructuring means the shares are now economically worth only 25 MAD.
- **Mental accounting:** Treating money differently based on its origin. An investor treats "trading profits" as "house money" and takes reckless risks with it, forgetting that every dirham in the account has the exact same purchasing power as hard-earned salary.

## How to neutralize cognitive bias: rules beat judgement

The primary takeaway from behavioural finance is that pure willpower cannot eliminate emotional bias. When markets plunge or surge, adrenaline and fear overwhelm rational calculation.

Professional Moroccan fund managers combat cognitive biases through **pre-committed systematic rules**:

1. **Pre-defined exit criteria:** Determine both your target profit level and your maximum loss threshold (*stop-loss*) before submitting an order. When the threshold is triggered, execution is mandatory and non-negotiable.
2. **Investment journaling:** Write down the explicit economic thesis before entering a position. When reviewing the holding months later, evaluate whether the fundamental thesis remains valid, rather than rationalizing price movements after the fact.
3. **Position sizing limits:** Cap any individual position at a fixed percentage of total portfolio capital (e.g., maximum 5% to 10%), ensuring that an adverse outcome cannot inflict fatal emotional or financial damage.

While our simulated trading platform allows club members to practice order execution without risking real dirhams, a simulator cannot replicate the visceral fear of losing real personal savings or the emotional greed of watching capital double. Club members must recognize that emotional discipline under paper trading is not identical to real-world psychological endurance.
