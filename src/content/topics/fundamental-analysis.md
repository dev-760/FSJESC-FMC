# Fundamental analysis: forecasting cash flows, not opinions

*For the members of the Financial Market Club, FSJES*

Fundamental analysis is the discipline of estimating what a business is intrinsically worth based on its underlying economic reality: the cash it generates, the assets it controls, and the durability of its competitive position.

Benjamin Graham, the father of value investing, established the foundational principle: in the short run, the stock market is a voting machine, driven by popularity and mood; but in the long run, it is a weighing machine, accurately reflecting the underlying cash flow generation of the enterprise.

## The three fundamental questions

Every rigorous fundamental analysis must answer three sequential questions:

1. **What does this business actually do to make money?** Not the corporate marketing slogan, but the economic engine. What products or services are sold? Who are the clients? What are the raw input costs? What barriers prevent competitors from stealing market share?
2. **How much cash will the business generate in the future?** Accounting profit is a construct of accounting conventions; actual cash deposited in the bank is what can be reinvested or paid out as dividends.
3. **What are those future cash flows worth today?** Discounting expected future cash flows at a rate of return that reflects the time value of money and the operational risk of the enterprise.

## Reading financial statements: the correct sequence

Under Moroccan law, companies listed on the Bourse de Casablanca publish annual and semi-annual financial statements governed by the Plan Comptable General Marocain (PCGM) and, for consolidated groups, International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), overseen by the AMMC.

Students typically make the mistake of reading the income statement (*compte de resultat*) first. A professional analyst follows a more rigorous sequence:

### 1. The Cash Flow Statement (*Tableau des Flux de Tresorerie*)
Start here. This statement shows actual cash movements across three activities:
- **Operating cash flows (Flux de tresorerie lie a l'exploitation):** Cash generated directly from core business operations.
- **Investing cash flows (Flux lie aux investissements):** Capital expenditure (*Capex*) required to maintain and expand industrial equipment, factories, and software.
- **Financing cash flows (Flux lie au financement):** Bank debt drawn or repaid, equity dividends distributed to shareholders.

**Free Cash Flow to the Firm (FCFF)** represents the true discretionary cash generated:

$$\text{Free Cash Flow} = \text{Operating Cash Flow} - \text{Capital Expenditures}$$

If a Moroccan industrial firm reports rising accounting net profits while operating cash flow is declining, it indicates that revenue is accumulating in uncollected customer receivables (*creances clients*) rather than actual cash in the bank.

### 2. The Balance Sheet (*Bilan*)
Next, inspect solvency and asset quality:
- **Working capital requirements (*Besoin en Fonds de Roulement - BFR*):** Does the company tie up excessive cash in inventory and slow-paying clients?
- **Net debt (*Endettement net*):** Compare total interest-bearing debt against EBITDA and equity. High financial leverage magnifies bankruptcy risk during economic contractions.

### 3. The Income Statement (*Compte de Resultat*)
Read this last. Evaluate gross margins, operating margins (*marge d'exploitation*), and return on invested capital (ROIC). Beware of one-off exceptional gains (*produits non courants*), such as selling real estate, which temporarily flatter net income without representing ongoing business earning power.

## Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) modeling

The standard institutional valuation methodology on the Casablanca financial market is the DCF model:

$$\text{Enterprise Value} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{\text{FCF}_t}{(1 + \text{WACC})^t} + \frac{\text{Terminal Value}}{(1 + \text{WACC})^n}$$

Where **WACC** is the Weighted Average Cost of Capital, combining the cost of debt (after tax) and the cost of equity derived from the Moroccan risk-free Treasury rate plus the market risk premium.

In mature Moroccan blue chips with wide economic moats (like Marsa Maroc with its long-term port concessions, or Cosumar with its integrated agricultural sugar network), cash flows are relatively predictable, making DCF analysis a robust valuation tool.
