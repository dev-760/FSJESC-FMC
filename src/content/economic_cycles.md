# Economic cycles: why conditions change together

Employment, output, credit and interest rates do not move independently. When one improves, the others usually follow. Mishkin defines business cycles as the upward and downward movement of aggregate output. A recession is a period of declining output.

### One mechanism, many effects

Mishkin's supply and demand model of the bond market shows how the pieces connect. Bonds are claims that firms sell to raise money and savers buy with their wealth. In an expansion, income and wealth rise, so demand for bonds shifts right. Businesses also find more profitable projects and want to borrow, so the supply of bonds shifts right too. In a recession, income and wealth fall and demand shifts left. Output, jobs and financial prices are linked through the same decisions.

### Morocco in 2015 and 2016

Al Maliya gives a clear case. Growth was 2.6% in 2014 and 4.5% in 2015. For 2016, the magazine expected a slowdown because of the cereal harvest. The estimate was 33.5 million quintals, the lowest since the launch of the Plan Maroc Vert, and agricultural GDP was expected to fall about 7.3%. Other farm products, especially olive production (up 25%), softened the blow.

Non-agricultural activity looked better in the first quarter of 2016: cement sales +5.9%, electricity output +3.5%, phosphate output +10.2%. The labour market was weaker. The economy lost 13,000 jobs between the first quarters of 2015 and 2016, and unemployment reached 10%, against 9.9% a year earlier. Agriculture, forestry and fishing lost 15,000 jobs.

This shows a point about cycles: sectors do not move in perfect step, but they are tied together through income. A poor harvest cuts rural earnings, and rural earnings feed into demand elsewhere. The magazine does not trace that last link, so treat it as the question to test, not a finding.

### What causes cycles?

Mishkin presents the monetary view: the growth rate of money has declined before every recession since the early twentieth century. He then adds the objections. The timing could reflect reverse causation, with the cycle driving the money supply. Real business cycle theorists argue that shocks to technology and tastes, not money, are the main driver. Mishkin's own reading is that the historical evidence on exogenous drops in money growth, such as the 1936 to 1937 reserve requirement increase followed by the 1937 to 1938 recession, supports the view that money matters, though not that it is all that matters.

For a Moroccan example of a non-monetary shock, the 2016 harvest fits the real-shock story.
