# Interest rates and how they reach prices

An interest rate is the price of using someone else's money. Like any price, it can be quoted in two ways, and only one of them tells you the real cost.

### Nominal and real

The nominal rate ignores inflation. The real rate subtracts expected inflation from it. Mishkin calls this the Fisher equation: real rate = nominal rate − expected inflation.

His two examples:

- A loan at 5% when you expect 3% inflation pays you 2% in real terms.
- A loan at 8% when you expect 10% inflation leaves the lender 2% *worse off* in real terms. The borrower gains 2%.

Mishkin's conclusion is that the real rate is the better guide to how people behave. When it is low, borrowers have more reason to borrow and lenders less reason to lend.

A rough Moroccan illustration, with a caution: Al Maliya reports that the weighted average rate on Treasury bills at the end of March 2016 was 2.7%, and inflation over the first quarter was 1%. That suggests a real return of about 1.7 points. This mixes a rate at one date with an inflation figure for a quarter, and it uses realized inflation instead of expected inflation. Treat it as a way to practice the arithmetic.

### From the central bank to the shop

Mishkin describes the traditional channel with a short chain: more money supply leads to a lower real interest rate, which lowers the cost of capital, which raises investment spending, which raises output. Consumer spending on housing and durable goods follows the same logic, since those purchases are investment decisions too.

A puzzle follows. Central banks control short-term *nominal* rates. Why would that change the real rate, and a long-term rate on top of that? Mishkin gives two reasons:

1. Prices are *sticky*. They adjust slowly, so a lower nominal rate also means a lower real rate for a while.
2. Long-term rates reflect an average of expected short-term rates, so lower short-term real rates pull long-term real rates down.

Mishkin also describes a second route through the exchange rate. Lower domestic real rates make domestic deposits less attractive, the currency weakens, domestic goods become cheaper abroad, and net exports rise. He adds a warning: researchers disagree on how strong the traditional channel is. John Taylor argues it is strong, while Bernanke and Gertler find weak evidence for strong effects through the cost of capital.

### Rates also move prices of securities

In the bond market, price and interest rate move in opposite directions. A discount bond with a face value of 1,000 sold at 950 yields (1,000 − 950) / 950, or 5.3%. At a price of 900, the yield is 11.1%. That is why articles about "falling rates" are also articles about rising bond prices.
